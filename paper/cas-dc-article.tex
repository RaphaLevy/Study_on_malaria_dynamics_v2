\begin{abstract}
%This template helps you to create a properly formatted \LaTeX\ manuscript.
This paper aims to analyze the behavior of malaria transmission in the Amazon region based on climatic and environmental changes, such as temperature, precipitation, humidity, deforestation and forest fires, through proposed modifications to the SIR and SEI models, in order to contribute to the study of applications of external effects on the evolution of the disease. 
The Trajetorias Project, developed by the Synthesis Center 
on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (SinBiose/CNPq) was used as an initial reference for the study. 
\\\\
This work employs a modified SEIRS/SEI methodology, based on the SIR/SEI model developed by Parham and Michael (2010) which takes into account rainfall and temperature, with further modifications. %Primary results show that the model is too sensible on some parameters, and using values indicated by other papers did not give the same results, opening up future work to compare the modified model equations with the originals. With the obtained results, it was possible to verify a strong effect caused by increased contact of host and vectors on the transmission of the disease. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Malaria remains one of the most significant vector-borne diseases worldwide, caused by 
protozoa of the genus \textit{Plasmodium} and transmitted by female mosquitoes of the 
genus \textit{Anopheles}. Although present in multiple regions, the burden of malaria in the Americas is 
highly concentrated in the Amazon basin, which spans nine countries and accounts for a substantial proportion of reported 
cases \cite{pimenta_orfano_bahia_duarte_rios-velasquez_melo_pessoa_oliveira_campos_villegas_etal_2015}. 
The persistence of malaria in this region is closely linked to ecological, climatic, and socioeconomic factors 
that influence both vector dynamics and human exposure.
\\\\
Environmental changes play a central role in shaping malaria transmission. In the Amazon, 
processes such as deforestation, agricultural expansion, and infrastructure development alter 
local ecosystems, affecting vector habitats and increasing human–vector contact. These changes often 
promote the proliferation of mosquito breeding sites and concentrate human populations in high-risk areas, 
particularly among non-immune migrants \cite{silva-nunes_malaria_amazon_2008, DASILVANUNES2012281}. 
In addition to land-use change, climatic variables such as temperature and rainfall, as well as disturbances such 
as forest fires and mining activities, further influence vector abundance, pathogen development, and transmission intensity.
\\\\
Despite the recognized importance of environmental drivers, integrating these factors into mathematical models of 
malaria transmission remains challenging. In particular, distinguishing between their effects on mosquito biology and 
their impact on human–vector interaction is essential for developing models that are both biologically consistent and 
identifiable from available data.
\\\\
In this work, we develop and analyze a modified SIR–SEI model for malaria transmission in the Amazon, incorporating 
environmental variables such as temperature, rainfall, deforestation, and fire activity. Climatic factors are modeled as 
drivers of mosquito life-history parameters, while land-use variables are introduced as modifiers of transmission intensity. 
Using epidemiological and environmental data from the Trajetorias Project \cite{Rorato2023}, we investigate how these 
factors influence disease dynamics and assess their relative contributions through analytical and numerical methods.

\section{Environmental Drivers of Malaria Transmission in the Amazon}
\label{sec:environmental_drivers}

Environmental changes, particularly deforestation and forest fires, have been widely associated with the 
resurgence and spatial redistribution of malaria transmission in the Amazon region \cite{Norris2004}. 
The removal of forest canopy alters local microclimatic conditions, increasing sunlight exposure and ambient temperature, 
which in turn favor the development of mosquito larvae and pupae. In particular, the primary malaria vector in the Amazon, 
\textit{Anopheles darlingi}, is known to thrive in partially deforested environments, where sunlit 
water bodies promote larval growth and increase the availability of food sources such as algae \cite{infoAnopheles, article_alteracoesambientais}.
\\\\
Beyond ecological effects, deforestation also influences human exposure to malaria. Activities such as logging, 
agriculture, and infrastructure development lead to increased human presence in recently altered environments, 
often in close proximity to mosquito breeding sites. This results in higher human–vector contact rates and facilitates 
transmission, especially in areas where both vector density and environmental suitability are elevated. Additionally, 
land-use changes such as agriculture may contribute to river sedimentation and the formation of artificial breeding sites, 
further enhancing mosquito proliferation.
\\\\
Forest fragmentation has been shown to accelerate mosquito development cycles by increasing temperature 
and light availability, reducing the time from larval to adult stages and increasing vectorial capacity and 
survivorship \cite{article_alteracoesambientais, Anthropogenic_Landscape}. Similarly, forest fires, often used as a 
land-clearing mechanism, remove vegetation and modify habitat structure, contributing to long-term ecological 
changes that can influence mosquito populations and disease transmission dynamics \cite{Future_of_Deforestation, Relationship_between_fires_and_defor}. 
These processes may also increase the likelihood of subsequent fires by promoting understory drying and fuel accumulation \cite{Conservation_Policy}.
\\\\
In this work, deforestation and fire activity are not embedded as mechanistic drivers within the 
epidemiological model itself. Instead, they are treated as environmental variables that describe the 
context in which malaria transmission occurs, and are used together with temperature, precipitation and 
humidity as predictors in the regression of the weekly transmission probabilities $b_1$ and $b_2$ (see 
Section~\ref{sec:freeing_transmission_coefficients}). In that methodological framework, the freed 
per-bite transmission probabilities $b_{1,w}$ and $b_{2,w}$, recovered from the observed case dynamics, 
are regressed against a set of environmental covariates that includes the land-cover and fire variables 
described below. This enables deforestation and fire activity to modulate transmission indirectly 
through the empirical relation between environmental conditions and the effective transmission 
coefficients, rather than by directly altering the mechanistic mosquito demographic or physiological 
rates.
\\\\
Deforestation was obtained from the DETER system, which provides daily records of deforestation alerts by degradation type, 
reported in km$^2$ \cite{Daily_DETER}. The daily alerts were aggregated and summarized at weekly resolution, 
with the 4-week total degraded area used as the deforestation covariate $D_f$ in the regression. Three degradation 
types were distinguished by DETER, namely clear cut, forest degradation and burn scar, and combined into a total 
deforestation term. Forest fire activity was obtained from two complementary sources. First, the Brazilian National 
Institute for Space Research (INPE) provides daily records of detected fire hotspots \cite{INPE_Forest_Fires}; because 
area-based measurements are not available at this temporal resolution, the weekly number of detected hotspots was 
used as a fire activity covariate $F_c$. Second, annual land-surface fire extent estimates from the MapBiomas 
project provide fire trends in hectares \cite{Mapbiomas}, assigned as a monthly covariate $F_t$ accounting for the burned area 
associated with fire activity.
\\\\
Daily climatic data, including temperature, precipitation and humidity, were obtained from the Mosqlimate API \cite{MosqlimateAPI} 
and included as the $T$, $R$ and $H$ covariates. Together, the variables $T, R, H, D_f, F_c, F_t$ form the 
environmental predictor set used to relate the observed environmental conditions to the weekly transmission 
probabilities $b_1$ and $b_2$.

\section{Model Formulation}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Baseline Delayed SIR-SEI Model}
\label{sec:baseline_delayed_sir_sei}

We begin with the classical host-vector framework for malaria transmission, based on the model introduced by Parham and Michael (2010) \cite{Parham2010}, 
which incorporates temperature- and rainfall-dependent mosquito dynamics. The human population is divided into susceptible, infected, 
and recovered compartments, denoted by $S_H$, $I_H$, and $R_H$, respectively. The mosquito population is structured into susceptible, 
exposed, and infectious compartments, denoted by $S_M$, $E_M$, and $I_M$.
\\\\
The transmission dynamics are governed by the system of delay differential equations:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:system}
\begin{align}
\frac{dS_H}{dt} &= - a b_2 \frac{I_M(t)}{N} S_H(t) \label{eq:systema} \\
\frac{dI_H}{dt} &= a b_2 \frac{I_M(t - \tau_H)}{N} S_H(t - \tau_H) - \gamma I_H(t) \label{eq:systemb} \\
\frac{dR_H}{dt} &= \gamma I_H(t) \label{eq:systemc} \\
\frac{dS_M}{dt} &= b(R,T) - \left(a b_1 \frac{I_H(t)}{N} + \mu(T)\right) S_M(t) \label{eq:systemd} \\
\frac{dE_M}{dt} &= a b_1 \frac{I_H(t)}{N} S_M(t) - \mu(T) E_M(t) \notag \\
&\quad - a b_1 \frac{I_H(t - \tau_M)}{N} S_M(t - \tau_M)\ell(\tau_M) \label{eq:systeme} \\
\frac{dI_M}{dt} &= a b_1 \frac{I_H(t - \tau_M)}{N} S_M(t - \tau_M)\ell(\tau_M) \notag \\
&\quad - \mu(T) I_M(t) \label{eq:systemf} 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\\
Here, $a(T)$ denotes the biting rate, $b_1$ and $b_2$ are transmission probabilities from humans to 
mosquitoes and vice versa, $\mu(T)$ is the mosquito mortality rate, and $\tau_H$, $\tau_M$ represent the intrinsic and 
extrinsic incubation periods. The function $b(R,T)$ describes mosquito recruitment, while $\ell(\tau_M)$ represents survival during the extrinsic incubation period.
\\\\
Although this formulation incorporates biologically relevant delays, numerical simulations revealed that, under 
the parameterization considered, the model fails to reproduce sustained transmission dynamics. In particular, 
both the infected human population and the mosquito population tends to extinction, indicating that the structure does not adequately capture endemic persistence.

\subsection{ODE approximation and structural limitations}
\label{sec:ode_approximation_limitations}

To simplify the analysis and avoid the technical complexity of delay differential equations, an ordinary differential equation (ODE) 
approximation was initially considered. In this formulation, the extrinsic incubation period in mosquitoes is modeled via a transition 
rate $b_3$, replacing the explicit delay $\tau_M$. %Additionally, human population dynamics are incorporated through a net recruitment rate $r_H$.
\\\\
The resulting system is given by:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:system}
\begin{align}
\frac{dS_H}{dt} &= - a b_2 \frac{I_M}{N} S_H \label{eq:systema} \\
\frac{dI_H}{dt} &= a b_2 \frac{I_M}{N} S_H - \gamma I_H \label{eq:systemb} \\
\frac{dR_H}{dt} &= \gamma I_H \label{eq:systemc} \\
\frac{dS_M}{dt} &= b(R,T) - \left(a b_1 \frac{I_H}{N} + \mu\right) S_M \label{eq:systemd} \\
\frac{dE_M}{dt} &= a b_1 \frac{I_H}{N} S_M - (\mu + b_3  \ell) E_M \label{eq:systeme} \\
\frac{dI_M}{dt} &= b_3 \ell E_M - \mu I_M \label{eq:systemf}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\\
While this formulation restores positivity and simplifies numerical implementation, it exhibits a 
fundamental limitation: the system tends toward a disease-free equilibrium after a single outbreak, failing to 
capture the recurrent nature of malaria transmission observed in endemic regions. This behavior highlights the 
importance of explicitly modeling incubation periods and immunity dynamics.

\subsection{SEIRS-SEI model with ODE approximation}
\label{sec:seirs_sei_model_ode_approximation}

To address the limitations of the previous formulations, we introduce an ODE based SEIRS-SEI model that incorporates 
both incubation delays as done above and temporary immunity in the human population. The human compartments 
are extended to include an exposed class $E_H$, and recovered individuals lose immunity at rate $\omega$, returning to the susceptible class.
\\\\
To account for incubation dynamics in both hosts and vectors, the transition parameters were defined as temperature-dependent development rates. 
\\\\
For humans, the intrinsic incubation process was modeled through
$b_{3H} = \frac{1}{\tau_H}$,
where $\tau_H$ denotes the intrinsic incubation period.
\\\\
For mosquitoes, the constant transition parameter $b_3 = 1/\tau_M$ from the original formulation was replaced by a Brière-type function,
\begin{align}
b_{3M}(T)=\eta T(T-T_{\min})(T_{\max}-T)^{1/2},
\end{align}
which incorporates minimum and maximum thermal thresholds for sporogonic development \cite{brierre_function_development_rate, ab_gumel_malaria_review}. 
\\\\
The scaling coefficient $\eta$ was estimated by imposing $b_{3M}(T_{\mathrm{opt}})=\frac{1}{\tau_M(T_{\mathrm{opt}})}$,
yielding
\begin{align}
\eta=
\frac{\frac{1}{\tau_M(T_{\mathrm{opt}})}}{
T_{\mathrm{opt}}
(T_{\mathrm{opt}}-T_{\min})
(T_{\max}-T_{\mathrm{opt}})^{1/2}
}
\end{align}
Another addition was the inclusion of a humidity-dependent survival component $p(H)$ on to the daily survival rate of the adult mosquitoes, which is modeled as a sigmoid function of the daily minimum relative humidity U. The parameter $H_0$ (in $\%$) represents the humidity threshold at which the mosquito experiences a 50$\%$ reduction in survival relative to its maximum, and $k$ (in $\%^{-1}$) controls the steepness of the transition. The parameter $\varphi$ (dimensionless) establishes a baseline survival floor, accounting for the persistence of individuals in favorable micro-habitats even under extreme desiccation conditions.
%\\\\
%Now, in order to properly estimate the parameters $T'$ and $D_1$ for the biting rate in regard to data from the Amazon
\\\\
The SEIRS-SEI complete model is then given by:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:system}
\begin{align}
\frac{dS_H}{dt} &= - \lambda_H(t) S_H + \omega R_H \label{eq:systema} \\
\frac{dE_H}{dt} &= \lambda_H(t) S_H - b_{3H} E_H \label{eq:systemb} \\
\frac{dI_H}{dt} &= b_{3H} E_H - \gamma I_H  \label{eq:systemc} \\
\frac{dR_H}{dt} &= \gamma I_H - \omega R_H  \label{eq:systemd} \\
\frac{dS_M}{dt} &= \Lambda(t)- \lambda_M(t) S_M - \mu S_M  \label{eq:systeme} \\
\frac{dE_M}{dt} &= \lambda_M(t) S_M - (\mu  + b_{3M}\ell) E_M \label{eq:systemf} \\
\frac{dI_M}{dt} &= b_{3M}\ell E_M - \mu I_M  \label{eq:systemg}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\\
The forces of infection are defined as:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:system}
\begin{align}
\lambda_H(t) &= a\, b_2 \frac{I_M(t)}{N(t)}  \label{eq:systema} \\
\lambda_M(t) &= a\, b_1 \frac{I_H(t)}{N(t)} \label{eq:systemb}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\subsection{Recalibration of the temperature-dependent biting rate}
\label{sec:recalibration_temp_dependent_biting_rate}
In the original formulation by Parham \& Michael \cite{Parham2010}, the biting rate is expressed as a temperature-dependent function derived from the duration of the gonotrophic cycle. Although this formulation captures the general thermal dependence of mosquito feeding behavior, its parameterization was not developed specifically for Amazonian mosquito populations. With this in mind, we recalibrated the biting-rate function using entomological observations reported by de Barros, Honório and Arruda \cite{debarros2011}, regarding females of \textit{Anopheles darlingi} collected between August 2003 and July 2004 through intermittent sampling carried out in selected months over the study period.
\\\\
Their results allowed for estimation of the duration of the gonotrophic cycle through the following formula:
\begin{align}
P=Q^{1/g} \Longrightarrow g = \frac{\ln(Q)}{\ln(P)},
\end{align}
where $P$ is the daily survival rate of mosquitoes, $Q$ is the proportion of parous mosquitoes females in the population and $g$ is the duration of the gonotrophic cycle, given in days  \cite{duration_of_gonotrophic_cycle}.
\\\\
Given the lack of more recent data, it was decided to use the obtained estimates of the time period specified above with average temperatures from the period of analysis between 2017 and 2023, given that the biting rate is given as a function of temperature. It was decided to consider $T'=16^\circ C$, given that below this temperature there is no development of the vector, as the threshold temperature for the biting rate, and we estimated values of $g$ for the missing months of the analysis via interpolation. With this, given the closed formula for the biting rate, and knowing that it is the inverse of the gonotrophic cycle, we calculated $D_1= g(T-T')$ for each of the months with available data \footnote{$a(T)$ is defined in Table A.1}.
\\\\
Having monthly values of $D_1$, we took their average in order to estimate the biting rate and the proportion of female mosquitoes to humans, which is given by
\begin{align}  
m = \frac{HBR}{a(T)},
\end{align}
where $HBR$ is the mean biting density of \textit{An. darlingi}, given in bites/person/night, while $a(T)$ is the biting rate, given in bites/mosquito/night. Based on the data, $M_0$, the initial population of mosquitos used in the analysis, was estimated to be
approximately $10 N_0$, based on the found value of $m$ for the month of January. 
\\\\
At this point, we also approximated the ratio of initially exposed to infectious mosquitoes to be their values at the steady state: $$\frac{dI_M}{dt}=b_{3M}\ell E_M - \mu I_M=0\Longrightarrow \frac{E(0)}{I(0)}=\frac{\mu(0,0)}{b_{3M}(0)\ell(0,0)}\approx3$$
\\\\
This adjustment preserves the functional structure proposed by Parham and Michael while adapting the parameter values to better reflect the feeding behavior of the primary malaria vector in the Amazon region.

\subsection{Density-dependent mosquito recruitment and carrying capacity}
\label{sec:density_dependent_recruitment_carrying_capacity}
Mosquito population dynamics were initially modeled using a linear birth–death process of the form
\begin{align}
\frac{dM}{dt} = b - \mu M,
\end{align}
where $b$ represents the birth rate and $\mu$ is the per capita mortality rate. 
However, this formulation does not account for environmental limitations on population growth.
\\\\
To incorporate the effect of limited environmental resources, we introduce a density-dependent recruitment mechanism inspired by the classical Verhulst logistic model \cite{math_assessment_of_the_role_of_temp_and_rainfall},
\begin{align}
\frac{dM}{dt} = bM\left(1 - \frac{M}{K}\right) - \mu M,
\end{align}
where $M(t)=S_M(t)+E_M(t)+I_M(t)$ denotes the total mosquito population, $b$ the per capita recruitment rate, $\mu$ the mortality rate, and $K$ the environmental carrying capacity. This formulation captures the reduction in population growth as the mosquito population approaches the environmental limit.
\\\\
In the present model, this idea is adapted by replacing the constant recruitment term with the density-dependent function
\begin{align}
\Lambda(t) = b(R,T)\,K(t)\left(1 - \frac{M(t)}{K(t)}\right),
\end{align}
where the environmental dependence is separated into two distinct mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The climatic modulation of mosquito recruitment through $b(R,T)$, inherited from the original Parham-Michael formulation;
\item The environmental limitation imposed by habitat availability through the carrying capacity $K(t)$.
\end{enumerate}
In this interpretation, $b(R,T)$ governs the rate at which mosquitoes are produced under favorable temperature and rainfall conditions, whereas $K(t)$ determines the maximum sustainable mosquito population supported by the environment. Consequently, temperature and precipitation affect mosquito dynamics both through reproductive success and through the availability of suitable breeding habitats.
\\\\
The environmental carrying capacity is defined by
\begin{align}
K(t) = M' \cdot f_T(T(t)) \cdot f_R(R(t)),
\end{align}
where $M'$ denotes the baseline mosquito population size. Given that we require $0\leq 1-\frac{M(t)}{K(t)}\leq 1$, we would require $M'$ such that $M'\geq \frac{M}{f_T(T)f_R(R)}$.%In the simulations, we assumed $M_0 = 35N_0$, with $N_0$ representing the initial human population. 
\\\\
The environmental response functions are given by
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:carryingcapacity}
\begin{align}
f_T(T) &= \min\biggl(1.0, 
\frac{
T(T-T_{\min\text{A.}})
\sqrt{T_{\max\text{A.}}-T}
}{
T_{\mathrm{op}\text{A.}}
(T_{\mathrm{op}\text{A.}}-T_{\min\text{A.}})
\sqrt{T_{\max\text{A.}}-T_{\mathrm{op}\text{A.}}}
}
\biggr), \label{eq:carryingcapacitya}
\\
f_R(R) &= 
\left(\frac{R}{R_L/2}\right)
\exp\left(1-\frac{R}{R_L/2}\right),
\label{eq:carryingcapacityb}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $f_T(T)$ describes the temperature suitability for mosquito persistence and $f_R(R)$ represents the dual effect of rainfall on habitat availability.
\\\\
The rainfall response function increases with precipitation under moderate rainfall conditions, reflecting the formation of breeding sites, but decreases under excessive rainfall due to habitat flushing and destruction of immature stages.
\\\\
Both environmental response functions are normalized so that $0 \leq f_T(T), f_R(R) \leq 1$, 
ensuring that the carrying capacity remains bounded by the baseline population scale $M_0$.
\\\\
Unlike the classical logistic formulation, where population growth is directly proportional to the current mosquito abundance, the present representation assumes that recruitment is primarily determined by environmental suitability and breeding-site availability. The factor
$\left(1-\frac{M(t)}{K(t)}\right)$ therefore imposes density-dependent regulation by reducing recruitment as the mosquito population approaches the carrying capacity, providing a biologically realistic upper bound on mosquito abundance. The mathematical implications of this formulation are analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:mosquito_pop_dynamics}.

\section{Data Description and Processing}
\label{sec:data_description_processing}

\subsection{Epidemiological Data}
\label{sec:epidemiological_data}

This study focuses on malaria cases caused by \textit{Plasmodium vivax}, which accounts for the majority 
of malaria infections in Brazil and is the predominant species in the Amazon 
region \cite{OliveiraFerreira2010, 10.3389/fpubh.2021.647754, Vector_Incrimination}. 
\\\\
Epidemiological data were obtained from the SIVEP-Malaria system \cite{SIVEP_Malaria}, 
which provides daily records of notified malaria cases for all Brazilian municipalities from 
2003 to 2023. The analysis was restricted to the period 2017–2023, for which complete epidemiological and environmental data were available.

\subsection{Population Estimates}
\label{sec:population_estimates}

The municipality of Manaus was selected due to its high incidence of \textit{P. vivax} malaria, 
particularly in rural areas. However, detailed yearly estimates of rural population were not directly available 
from census data. To address this limitation, population estimates were derived using data from the 
Trajetorias Project \cite{Rorato2023}, which provides incidence values aggregated over five-year periods.
\\\\
Incidence is defined as
\begin{align}
\text{Inc}(d,m,z,t_1,t_2)
=
\frac{\text{Cases}(d,m,z,t_1,t_2)}
{\text{Pop}(m,z,(t_1+t_2)/2)\cdot (t_2-t_1)}
\times 10^5,
\end{align}
where $\text{Cases}(d,m,z,t_1,t_2)$ denotes the number of reported cases for 
disease $d$ in municipality $m$ and zone $z$ over the interval $[t_1,t_2]$, 
and $\text{Pop}(m,z,\cdot)$ represents the population at the midpoint of the interval.
\\\\
This relationship was inverted to estimate rural population values from reported incidence and case counts. 
Using this approach, the rural population of Manaus was estimated to represent less than $1\%$ of the total population, ranging between $0.3\%$ and $0.6\%$ 
of the total municipal population, while accounting for a disproportionately large share of malaria cases (approximately $30\%$ to $40\%$ over the study periods).
\\\\
Yearly total population values for Manaus were obtained via interpolation based on official census data from 
the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) for the years 2000, 2010, and 
2022 \cite{Census2000, Census2010, Census2022}. Intermediate yearly values were constructed using 
linear interpolation, while values beyond 2022 were extrapolated based on observed trends.

\subsection{Data Consistency Adjustments}
\label{sec:data_consistency_adjustments}

During preprocessing, inconsistencies were identified in the Trajetorias dataset. In particular, 
incidence rates for the period 2015–2019 had been calculated using population estimates corresponding 
to 2004–2008, resulting in a temporal mismatch. These values were recalculated using population 
estimates consistent with the appropriate time interval.
\\\\
Additionally, rural and urban population estimates for intermediate years had been derived using fixed 
proportions based on 2010 data. To better account for demographic changes, particularly urbanization, 
these estimates were revised. The rural population for 2006 was approximated as the mean between the 
reported values for 2000 and 2010, while the 2017 value was obtained through extrapolation based on the 
observed growth trend. These adjustments ensured temporal consistency in the population data used throughout the study. 

\section{Mathematical Analysis of the Model}
\label{sec:mathematical_analysis_of_model}

\subsection{Mosquito population dynamics}
\label{sec:mosquito_pop_dynamics}
In this section, we analyze the qualitative impact of the density-dependent recruitment term on mosquito population dynamics. 
We compare the proposed formulation with the classical Verhulst model and show that the modified structure prevents extinction under suitable environmental conditions.

\begin{proposition}[Environmental persistence of the mosquito population]
\label{prop:persistence}
Assume that $b(R(t),T(t)) \geq b_0 > 0$ and $K(t) \geq K_0 > 0$ for all $t \geq 0$. 
Then, the mosquito population $M(t)$ governed by
\begin{align}
\frac{dM}{dt} = b(R,T)\,K(t)\left(1 - \frac{M(t)}{K(t)}\right) - \mu M(t)
\end{align}
\\
cannot converge to zero as $t \to \infty$. In particular, $M=0$ is not an equilibrium and cannot be asymptotically stable.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We begin by recalling the classical logistic model with mortality:
\begin{align}
\frac{dM}{dt} = bM\left(1 - \frac{M}{K}\right) - \mu M.
\end{align}
\\
In this formulation, the right-hand side vanishes at $M=0$, so that $M=0$ is always an equilibrium. 
Depending on the parameters, this equilibrium may be stable, allowing extinction of the population.
\\\\
In contrast, in the present model the recruitment term is given by
\begin{align}
\Lambda(t) = b(R,T)\,K(t)\left(1 - \frac{M(t)}{K(t)}\right),
\end{align}
\\
and the population dynamics satisfy
\begin{align}
\frac{dM}{dt} = b(R,T)\,K(t)\left(1 - \frac{M(t)}{K(t)}\right) - \mu M(t).
\end{align}
\\
Evaluating the right-hand side at $M=0$, we obtain
\begin{align}
\left.\frac{dM}{dt}\right|_{M=0} = b(R,T)\,K(t).
\end{align}
\\
By assumption, $b(R(t),T(t)) \geq b_0 > 0$ and $K(t) \geq K_0 > 0$, hence
\begin{align}
\left.\frac{dM}{dt}\right|_{M=0} \geq b_0 K_0 > 0.
\end{align}
\\
Therefore, $M=0$ is not an equilibrium of the system.
\\\\
Moreover, for $M(t)$ sufficiently small, we have
\begin{align}
\frac{dM}{dt} \geq b_0 K_0 - \mu M(t).
\end{align}
Choosing $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $\varepsilon < \frac{b_0 K_0}{\mu}$, it follows that
\[
\frac{dM}{dt} > 0 \quad \text{whenever } 0 < M(t) < \varepsilon.
\]
Thus, any trajectory entering a neighborhood of zero is driven away from it.
\\\\
Consequently, $M(t)$ cannot converge to zero as $t \to \infty$, and the extinction state is not asymptotically stable.
\end{proof}

Following the next-generation matrix method of van den Driessche and Watmough \cite{Rep_Number_Comp_Model}, 
we order the infected compartments as
$X_I = (E_H, I_H, E_M, I_M)^T$
and denote by $\mathcal{F}_i(x)$ the rate of appearance of new infections
in compartment $i$ and by $\mathcal{V}_i(x) = \mathcal{V}_i^-(x) - \mathcal{V}_i^+(x)$
the net rate of transfer of individuals into and out of compartment $i$.
The disease transmission model consists of non-negative initial conditions
together with the system
$\dot{x} = f(x) = \mathcal{F}(x) - \mathcal{V}(x)$,

\paragraph{Disease-free equilibrium:}
The disease-free equilibrium (DFE) is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dfe}
\begin{aligned}
x_0 &= (S_H^*, E_H^*, I_H^*, R_H^*, S_M^*, E_M^*, I_M^*)^\top \\
    &= (N, 0, 0, 0, M^*, 0, 0)^\top .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The equilibrium mosquito abundance $M^*$ is obtained from the mosquito subsystem
\begin{equation}
\frac{dM}{dt} = \Lambda(t) - \mu M = b(R,T)K(t)\left(1 - \frac{M}{K(t)}\right) - \mu M = 0,
\end{equation}
which yields
\begin{equation}
M^* = \frac{\bar{b}\,\bar{K}}{\bar{b}+\mu}, \qquad
\Lambda^* := \Lambda\big|_{M=M^*} = \mu M^* = \frac{\mu\bar{b}\,\bar{K}}{\bar{b}+\mu},
\label{eq:lambda_star}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{b}=b(\bar{R},\bar{T})$ and $\bar{K}=K(t)$ are the environmental drivers
evaluated at a fixed reference state, that being their study-period means, so that
$\mathcal{R}_0$ is a function of that reference climate-habitat state. Note that
$\Lambda^*$ is \emph{not} an arbitrary value: it is the recruitment rate that
sustains the equilibrium vector population $M^*$, and it inherits the environmental
dependence of $b(R,T)$ and $K(t)$.

\paragraph{Next-generation matrices:}
The new-infection and transfer matrices, evaluated at the DFE, are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
F &= \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 & 0 & \dfrac{ab_2 S_H^*}{N} \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \dfrac{ab_1 S_M^*}{N} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \\[6pt]
V &= \begin{pmatrix}
b_{3H} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
-b_{3H} & \gamma & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & b_{3M}\ell + \mu & 0 \\
0 & 0 & -b_{3M}\ell & \mu
\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ngm}
\end{equation}
where we used $S_H^* = N$ and $S_M^* = M^*$ at the DFE. The spectral radius of $FV^{-1}$ gives
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_0 = \rho(FV^{-1}) = \sqrt{\frac{a^2 b_1 b_2 \Lambda^* b_{3M}\ell}{N\gamma(b_{3M}\ell+\mu)\mu^2}},
\label{eq:R0}
\end{equation}
which can be written in the classical two-factor host-vector form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{R}_0^H &= \frac{ab_2}{\mu}; \ \ \mathcal{R}_0^M = \frac{ab_1\Lambda^*}{N\mu\gamma}\cdot\frac{b_{3M}\ell}{b_{3M}\ell+\mu} \\
\Longrightarrow \mathcal{R}_0 &= \sqrt{\mathcal{R}_0^H \cdot \mathcal{R}_0^M},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:R0_factors}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}_0^H$ is the expected number of humans infected by a single
infectious mosquito (mosquito infectious lifetime $1/\mu$), and $\mathcal{R}_0^M$ is
the expected number of mosquitoes that survive the latent period and become
infectious, generated by a single infectious human (human infectious period $1/\gamma$).
The geometric mean arises from the two-generation host-vector transmission cycle:
the infection must complete a full human$\rightarrow$mosquito$\rightarrow$human loop,
and the block structure of the next-generation matrix decouples into these two factors (see Appendix~\ref{app:ngm_verify} for the explicit
multiplication).
\\\\
We note that $\mathcal R_0$ is independent of both the intrinsic
incubation period $\tau_H$ and the immunity waning rate $\omega$:
the former because the model has no human disease-induced mortality
(every exposed individual eventually becomes infectious, so the
latent period affects only the timing of secondary infections), and
the latter because no individuals are immune at the disease-free
equilibrium, so waning is irrelevant to the linearized invasion
dynamics.

\section{Computational Implementation}
\label{sec:computational_implemetation}

\subsection{Objective function and parameter bounds}
\label{sec:objective_function}

Model parameters were estimated by minimizing the discrepancy between the simulated infected-human trajectory $I_H(t)$ and the observed active-case counts reported through SIVEP-Malaria, restricted to rural Manaus (scaled by the rural share of reported cases, 41.54$\%$). The objective function is a weighted mean squared error (wMSE):
\begin{align}
\text{wMSE}(\theta) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \left( I_H(t_i;\theta) - y_i \right)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i},
\label{eq:wmse}
\end{align}
where $y_i$ denotes the observed active-case count at date $t_i$, $I_H(t_i;\theta)$ is the model prediction obtained by linear interpolation of the simulated trajectory onto the observation dates, and $\theta$ is the vector of calibrated parameters.
\\\\
The weights $w_i$ follow an exponential decay that prioritizes early-time dynamics within each year:
\begin{align}
w_i = w_0 \, \exp\!\left(-\lambda \, d_i\right),
\label{eq:weight}
\end{align}
where $d_i$ is the number of days from the start of the year for observation $i$, $w_0 = 5.0$ is the initial weight, and $\lambda = 0.0015$ \textrm{day}$^{-1}$ is the decay rate. This weighting scheme reflects the greater sensitivity of the early-season trajectory to initial conditions and parameter values, guiding the optimizer toward solutions that correctly capture the onset of the rainy-season transmission peak.
\\\\
To prevent the optimizer from collapsing the infected-mosquito population to near-zero levels---which would sever the force-of-infection coupling and render the human transmission parameters structurally unidentifiable---an extinction penalty was added to the objective:
\begin{align}
J(\theta) = \text{wMSE}(\theta) + \mathcal{P}\!\left(I_M^{\min}(\theta)\right),
\label{eq:combined}
\end{align}
where $I_M^{\min}(\theta) = \min_{t} I_M(t;\theta)$ is the annual minimum of the infected-mosquito compartment, and
\begin{align}
\mathcal{P}\!\left(I_M^{\min}\right) = \begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } I_M^{\min} \geq I_M^*, \\[4pt]
\mathcal{B} \left(1 + \dfrac{I_M^* - I_M^{\min}}{I_M^*}\right) & \text{if } I_M^{\min} < I_M^*,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:penalty}
\end{align}
with $I_M^* = 10$ (viability threshold) and $\mathcal{B} = 10^8$ (penalty base scale). The penalty scales linearly with the depth of extinction, ensuring that partially-extinct trajectories are ranked proportionally rather than receiving a uniform penalty that would flatten the objective landscape.

The nine calibrated parameters were grouped into four biological ``topics'' for sequential fitting:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Humidity response} $(H_0,\, k,\, \varphi)$: the half-saturation threshold, sigmoid steepness, and baseline survival floor of the humidity-dependent survival function $p(H)$;
\item \textbf{Human transmission} $(\tau_H,\, \gamma,\, \omega)$: the intrinsic incubation period, recovery rate, and immunity waning rate;
\item \textbf{Force of infection} $(b_1,\, b_2)$: the human-to-mosquito and mosquito-to-human transmission probabilities;
\item \textbf{Carrying capacity} $(M')$: the baseline mosquito population scale that determines the environmental carrying capacity $K(t)$.
\end{enumerate}

The parameter bounds are given in Table~\ref{tbl:bounds}. The lower bound on $H_0 = 20\%$ prevents the humidity response from collapsing (which would drive $\mu \to \infty$ and extinguish the vector population). The bound on $b_2 \geq 0.01$ ensures a minimum level of mosquito-to-human transmission, while $\tau_H \in [10, 20]$ days constrains the human incubation period to the biologically plausible range for \textit{P. vivax} \cite{brasil_et_al_2011_unexpectedly_long_incubation_period_of_plasmodium_vivax}.

\subsection{Year-by-year sequential calibration}
\label{sec:year_by_year_calibration}

Parameters were calibrated sequentially for each year from 2017 to 2023 using the \texttt{lmfit} implementation of differential evolution (DE) \cite{storn_price_1997}. The DE settings were: population multiplier $\texttt{popsize} = 6$, maximum function evaluations $\texttt{max\_nfev} = 800$, convergence tolerance $\texttt{tol} = 0.01$, and random seed fixed at 42 for reproducibility.
\\\\
Within each year, the four topics were fitted in the fixed order:
\begin{align}
\underbrace{(H_0,\, k,\, \varphi)}_{\text{humidity}} \;\to\; \underbrace{(\tau_H,\, \gamma,\, \omega)}_{\text{human}} \;\to\; \underbrace{(b_1,\, b_2)}_{\text{FoI}} \;\to\; \underbrace{M'}_{\text{capacity}}
\label{eq:topic_order}
\end{align}
At each step, only the current topic's parameters were free; all previously fitted parameters were held fixed at their most recent estimates. This sequential strategy reduces the dimensionality of each optimization sub-problem while respecting the biological ordering: the humidity response determines mosquito mortality, which constrains the infection cycle, which in turn determines the force of infection and carrying capacity.
\\\\
After the sequential pass, a joint refinement was performed using the Nelder-Mead simplex method \cite{nelder_mead_1965}, with all nine parameters free simultaneously, seeded from the sequential solution. A seed-evaluation guard was applied: after each DE run and after each refinement stage, the starting point was explicitly evaluated, and the optimizer's output was accepted only if it improved upon the seed. This prevents the stochastic DE from returning a solution worse than its initialization.

The model was calibrated sequentially across years, with the end-state vector of each year serving as the initial state for the next:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{z}_0^{(y+1)} = \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{end}}^{(y)},
\label{eq:chain}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{z} = (S_H,\, E_H,\, I_H,\, R_H,\, S_M,\, E_M,\, I_M)$ is the full state vector. The human infected compartment was always reset to the first observed active-case count for the new year:
\begin{align}
{I_H}^{(y+1)}_0 = \mathrm{round}\!\left(y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\text{first}}\right),
\label{eq:ih_reset}
\end{align}
while all other compartments were carried forward without modification. Negative end-state values were clamped to zero before carrying.
\\\\
For the first year (2017), initial-condition fractions were explicitly fitted using Nelder-Mead with 300 function evaluations:
\begin{align}
S_{H,0} = \alpha_S \cdot N, \quad E_{H,0} = \alpha_E \cdot N, \quad I_{M,0} = \alpha_I \cdot M_0,
\label{eq:ic_fitted}
\end{align}
where $\alpha_S \in [0.3,\, 0.9]$, $\alpha_E \in [0.001,\, 0.30]$, and $\alpha_I \in [0.0005,\, 0.15]$, with the remaining compartments determined by conservation: $R_{H,0} = N - S_{H,0} - E_{H,0} - I_{H,0}$, $M_0 = 10N$, and $S_{M,0} = M_0 - E_{M,0} - I_{M,0}$.

\subsection{Weekly beta diagnostic}
\label{sec:weekly_beta_diagnostic}

To evaluate where the climate-driven formulation deviates from what the data demands, a diagnostic calibration was performed in which the effective transmission coefficients were freed as per-week parameters instead of being derived from the biting rate and fixed $b_1$, $b_2$:
\begin{align}
\lambda_H(t) = \beta_{h,w} \, \frac{I_M(t)}{N}, \qquad \lambda_M(t) = \beta_{m,w} \, \frac{I_H(t)}{N},
\label{eq:weekly_beta}
\end{align}
where $w = \lfloor d / 7 \rfloor$ is the week index and $d$ is the day number within the year. This yields $2 \times n_{\text{weeks}}$ free parameters per year (typically 106 for a 365-day year), with bounds $\beta_{h,w},\, \beta_{m,w} \in [10^{-9},\, 3.0]$.
\\\\
All other model parameters were fixed at the values obtained from the yearly topic calibration. The initial state was reconstructed from the previous year's end-state, with $I_H$ reset to observed data. The optimizer used was \texttt{lmfit.minimize} with the trust-region reflective method (\texttt{least\_squares}), with $\texttt{max\_nfev} = 1200$ and $\texttt{ftol} = 10^{-8}$.
\\\\
The residual vector for the trust-region solver was constructed as:
\begin{align}
r_i = \sqrt{w_i}\left(I_H(t_i) - y_i\right) + \sqrt{\mathcal{P}\!\left(I_M^{\min}\right)},
\label{eq:weekly_residual}
\end{align}
where $\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}$ is injected as a constant offset to preserve the residual vector length while providing a gradient that pushes $I_M$ upward when the mosquito population approaches the viability threshold.
\\\\
The weekly beta fit serves as a diagnostic baseline: it reveals the best achievable fit when the transmission coefficients are unconstrained by the climate formulation. Persistent deviations between the weekly-optimal $\beta_{h,w}$ and the climate-driven $a(T) \cdot b_2$ indicate periods where the environmental model fails to capture the true transmission dynamics.

\subsection{Freeing the transmission coefficients}
\label{sec:freeing_transmission_coefficients}

There are three main observations that allow us to use the effective transmission coefficients $\beta_{h,w}$ and $\beta_{m,w}$ as per-week free parameters:
\\\\
\textbf{(i) The transmission coefficients are intrinsically time-varying:} In the mechanistic formulation, the forces of infection are given by $\lambda_H = a(T)\,b_2\, I_M/N$ and $\lambda_M = a(T)\,b_1\, I_H/N$, where the biting rate $a(T)$ is the only environmental factor and the per-bite probabilities $b_1$ and $b_2$ are assumed to be constants. This assumption is biologically restrictive because these coefficients summarize several processes that may vary over the transmission season, including parasite development within the vector, vector susceptibility to infection, vector to host contact, and host infectiousness. These processes can be affected by temperature, rainfall, humidity, and changes in vector and host demography \cite{brierre_function_development_rate, human_to_mosquito_transmission_efficiency}. We therefore relax the assumption that $b_1$ and $b_2$ remain constant throughout the study period and allow the data to determine how transmission changes over time.
\\\\
\textbf{(ii) The weekly fit provides a nonparametric estimate of the latent transmission process:} We represent the effective transmission coefficients as latent time-varying functions, $\beta_h(t)$ and $\beta_m(t)$. These functions are approximated as piecewise constant over weekly intervals: $$\beta_h(t)=\beta_{h,w},\ \beta_m(t)=\beta_{m,w},\ t\in[7w,7(w+1))$$
This provides a flexible finite-dimensional representation of possible transmission profiles. The weekly coefficients are estimated by minimizing the weighted residual between the model predictions and the observed malaria cases. Thus, rather than imposing a specific relationship between transmission and environmental covariates, the weekly model provides a flexible, data-driven estimate of the temporal variation in transmission.
\\\\
The resulting $2\times n_{\mathrm{weeks}}$ coefficients don't represent independent biological parameters to be estimated, but rather provide a flexible empirical representation of the transmission process. The additional degrees of freedom allow the model to capture temporal changes in transmission that a more constrained model may miss, providing a reference for evaluating the proposed mechanistic model.
\\\\
\textbf{(iii) The climate-driven model is nested within the weekly model:} The climate-driven model is obtained by imposing the restriction
\begin{align}
\beta_{h,w} = a(T_w) b_2, \qquad \beta_{m,w} = a(T_w)\, b_1,
\label{eq:nested_beta}
\end{align}
where $b_1$ and $b_2$ are constant over the entire study period. Consequently, the climate-driven formulation is a restricted case of the more flexible weekly model. Because all other model parameters and initial conditions are held fixed, the difference in fit between the two formulations directly measures the extent to which the constant-coefficient assumption limits the model's ability to reproduce the observed cases.
\\\\
Releasing the coefficients reduces the wMSE by one to two orders of magnitude relative to the climate-driven model. This substantial improvement indicates that the data contain temporal variation in transmission that cannot be adequately represented by constant 
$b_1$ and $b_2$.
\\\\
The weekly estimates are therefore used as a diagnostic benchmark rather than as the final parameterization of the model. They identify the temporal transmission pattern that a parsimonious mechanistic formulation should seek to explain. This approach preserves the distinction between estimating a flexible empirical transmission process and identifying the underlying biological parameters, as the weekly coefficients characterize the observed transmission signal, while the subsequent structural model determines how that signal can be explained by environmental drivers.
\\\\
Finally, the weekly estimates provide the link between the empirical transmission signal and the environmental model. Since the biting rate $a(T_w)$ is explicitly modeled as a function of temperature, the corresponding weekly per-bite transmission probabilities can be recovered as
\begin{align}
b_{1,w}=\frac{\beta_{m,w}}{a(T_w)},
\qquad
b_{2,w}=\frac{\beta_{h,w}}{a(T_w)}.
\label{eq}
\end{align}
These time-varying estimates represent the per-bite transmission probabilities implied by the observed case dynamics after accounting for the temperature-dependent biting rate. They are subsequently used as empirical targets for regression models relating $b_1$ and $b_2$ to environmental drivers. In this way, the weekly fit serves as an intermediate step in the modeling chain 
$$ \text{environment} \longrightarrow (b_1,b_2) \longrightarrow \text{transmission} \longrightarrow \text{cases}, $$
allowing a flexible, data-driven estimate of transmission to be translated into a simplified and mechanistically interpretable environmental model.


\subsection{Numerical solver and implementation}
\label{sec:numerical_solver_implementation}

All ODE simulations were performed using Python version 3.12.7 (conda-forge distribution), using the LSODA solver as implemented in \texttt{scipy.integrate.solve\_ivp} (SciPy version 1.17.1), with automatic stiffness detection and order switching. The integration was evaluated on a fine grid of 10 points per day ($\Delta t = 0.1$ day), with the solution interpolated back to daily resolution via linear interpolation. End-state vectors were clamped to zero to prevent negative compartment values from propagating through the year chain.
\\\\
Climate inputs (temperature, precipitation, minimum humidity) were smoothed using a 14-day rolling mean for mosquito demographic rates (mortality $\mu$, sporogonic development $b_{3M}$, birth rate $\Lambda$), while the biting rate $a(T)$ used raw daily temperature to preserve high-frequency transmission variability.
\\\\
Data manipulation and analysis were performed using Pandas version 3.0.2,  while numerical computations and array operations were carried out with  NumPy version 2.4.2.
\\\\
All computations were performed on a laptop running Windows 11 (64-bit) equipped with a 12th Gen Intel Core i5-1235U processor (10 cores, 12 threads) and 16\,GB RAM. %The source code and calibration results are available at \url{https://github.com/RaphaLevy/Study_on_malaria_dynamics_v2}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Reproduction of the original Parham \& Michael model as a negative baseline}
\label{sec:results_parham_michael}
As a diagnostic for the methodological choices adopted below, the original SIR-SEI formulation of Parham and Michael (2010) \cite{Parham2010} was implemented with all parameter values reported in the original study, while population sizes and initial epidemiological conditions were estimated from the available rural Manaus data (Appendix Fig.~\ref{FIG:1A}). Under this parameterization the infected human population declines monotonically and the mosquito population rapidly collapses to extinction, indicating that mosquito recruitment is insufficient to replace mortality. Because transmission depends on the persistence of infectious vectors, the collapse of the vector population eliminates infection in both host and vector populations. Although the delay formulation preserved positivity, it fails to reproduce the endemic behavior expected for malaria in the Amazon, confirming the need to recalibrate the environmental and transmission parameters as described in Section~\ref{sec:recalibration_temp_dependent_biting_rate} and Section~\ref{sec:year_by_year_calibration}.

\subsection{Recalibration of the temperature-dependent biting rate}
\label{sec:results_biting_rate}
The temperature-dependent biting rate was recalibrated against field estimates of \textit{Anopheles darlingi} feeding behavior following Section~\ref{sec:recalibration_temp_dependent_biting_rate}. The recalibrated function yielded a minimum biting threshold of $T' = 16\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and a thermal scaling factor $D_1 = 24.8\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{days}$. Compared with the original Parham \& Michael parameterization, the refit shifts the biting response toward the thermal range observed in the Amazon and modifies its sensitivity near the transmission window. All subsequent simulations and the weekly transmission regressions of Section~\ref{sec:results_regression} make use of this recalibrated biting rate.

\subsection{Model calibration reproduces the observed transmission dynamics}
\label{sec:results_calibration}
The SEIRS-SEI model was calibrated year by year over 2017-2023 following the sequential topic scheme of Section~\ref{sec:year_by_year_calibration}, and the recovered parameters are summarized in Table~\ref{tbl:calibration}. Figure~\ref{FIG:model_vs_data} shows the fitted infectious compartment $I_H$ against the observed active-case count alongside the weekly free-beta fit for 2017, which exhibited the largest relative improvement of the weekly model over the constant-coefficient formulation (Section~\ref{sec:results_weekly_beta}). The calibrated parameters remain within their physiologically prescribed bounds found in Table~\ref{tbl:bounds}, with the humidity response ($H_0,\,k,\,\varphi$) and the human recovery parameters ($\tau_H,\,\gamma,\,\omega$) showing limited cross-year variation, while the force-of-infection coefficients $b_1$ and $b_2$ consistently approach their upper and lower bounds, respectively (Table~\ref{tbl:calibration}).

\subsection{The constant-coefficient climate model underfits the observed dynamics}
\label{sec:results_weekly_beta}
To assess how much of the seasonal variation in transmission is captured by the mechanistic, climate-driven formulation, we freed the effective transmission coefficients, allowing them to vary on a weekly basis (Section~\ref{sec:freeing_transmission_coefficients}), and compared the resulting weekly model with the constant-coefficient climate-driven model. Releasing the coefficients reduced the weighted mean squared error in every year (Table~\ref{tbl:weekly_beta}), most notably in 2017, where the wMSE fell from  $8.5\times10^4$ to $6.2\times10^2$ (a $\sim$137-fold reduction).
\\\\
The residual between the free weekly $\beta_{h,w},\beta_{m,w}$ and the climate-driven $a(T)b_1,\,a(T)b_2$ indicates periods in which the temperature-driven formulation fails to capture the observed transmission structure. This motivates extending the per-bite transmission probabilities to the wider set of environmental drivers introduced in Section~\ref{sec:environmental_drivers}.

\subsection{Environmental regression of the transmission probabilities $b_1$ and $b_2$}
\label{sec:results_regression}
Following the framework of Section~\ref{sec:environmental_drivers}, the freed weekly per-bite transmission probabilities were regressed against the environmental predictors $T,\,R,\,H,\,D_f,\,F_c,\,F_t$ (temperature, precipitation, humidity, deforestation, fire counts and fire trends) fitting an independent random forest for each year.
\\\\
Within each year the model explains a substantial share of the variance of both transmission probabilities, with mean $R^2 = 0.685 \pm 0.082$ for $b_1$ and mean $R^2 = 0.784 \pm 0.130$ for $b_2$, with near-zero prediction bias (|bias| $\lesssim$ 0.003 in every year; Table~\ref{tbl:regression}, Fig.~\ref{FIG:imp_b2}).

A leave-one-year-out cross-validation of the same forests qualifies this result. This contrast confirms that the environmental drivers are strongly year-specific: the within-year seasonal trajectory of $T,R,H,D_f,F_c,F_t$ shapes each year's transmission signal, whereas a fixed, year-independent link between the environment and $b_1,b_2$ is not supported by the data.

\subsection{The model sustains endemic transmission where the baseline does not}
\label{sec:baseline_doesnt_sustain_transmission}
The transition from the delayed SIR-SEI model of Parham and Michael to the ODE-based SEIRS-SEI model was not simply a numerical convenience. As shown in Section~\ref{sec:baseline_delayed_sir_sei}, the original parameterization leads to the extinction of both infected humans and mosquitoes, since the linear mosquito recruitment term is not sufficient to compensate for mosquito mortality under the environmental conditions of Manaus. In the modified model, the explicit extrinsic incubation delay is replaced by the transition rate $b_{3M}\ell$, while mosquito recruitment is made density-dependent. These changes allow the model to sustain the mosquito population and reproduce endemic transmission, as established in Proposition~\ref{prop:persistence}. Thus, the modifications change the qualitative behavior of the system and are important for obtaining a model that is consistent with the observed persistence of malaria transmission in Manaus.

\subsection{Constant coefficients underfit: the transmission
probabilities are seasonally heterogeneous}
\label{sec:constant_coeff_underfit}

The constant-coefficient model consistently underestimates the
observed dynamics, as the reduction in the weighted mean squared error
between the free-weekly and constant formulations demonstrates
(Table~\ref{tbl:weekly_beta}). The improvement ranges from a
$\sim$137-fold reduction in 2017 to a $\sim$2.4-fold reduction in
2023, indicating that the constant-coefficient formulation already
captures most of the signal in the latter year, while the transmission
probabilities vary more strongly across weeks in the former. This
heterogeneity is the empirical signature of the environmental
dependence of transmission, and it motivates the week-specific
regression of $b_{1,w}$ and $b_{2,w}$ in
Section~\ref{sec:results_regression}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the population at
risk $N_y$ was taken as the rural population of Manaus. The
inter-census estimates for this quantity were provided by the
Trajetórias Project \cite{Rorato2023}, which derives them from fixed
rural-urban proportions based on the 2010 census. While this approach
ensures internal consistency, it does not account for the marked
demographic changes in Manaus over time, particularly the rapid
urbanization observed in the region. To mitigate this limitation, the
rural proportions used in this study were recalculated (see
Section~\ref{sec:data_consistency_adjustments}) by interpolating and
extrapolating the available census data, providing a more realistic
representation of the temporal evolution of the rural population. This
adjustment is relevant because rural areas contribute
disproportionately to malaria incidence. Nevertheless, the resulting
decrease in $N_y$ during 2017-2023 reflects a demographic trend that
may still not correspond to the actual exposure of the human population
to mosquitoes. Second, the environmental drivers are treated as
deterministic and observed; unobserved covariates, such as vector
control interventions or local mobility, are not represented and may
contribute to the between-season degradation of the random forest.
Third, the model is fitted to reported case counts, which may be
subject to reporting bias and under-reporting, particularly in the
lowest-transmission months.

\subsection{Derivation of the basic reproduction number}
\label{app:r0_derivation}

This appendix details the next-generation matrix (NGM) computation
summarised in Section~\ref{sec:basic_reproduction_number}. The infected
compartments are ordered as $(E_H, I_H, E_M, I_M)$, so $m = 4$, and all
parameters are as defined in Section~3.

\paragraph{Disease-free states and the NGM formalism:}
\label{app:ngm_formalism}
Following van den Driessche and Watmough \cite{Rep_Number_Comp_Model},
let $X_s$ be the set of all disease-free states:
$$X_s = \{x \geq 0 \mid x_i = 0,\ i = 1, \ldots, m\},$$
where $X = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)^\top$, $x_i \geq 0$ is the number of
individuals in compartment $i$, and every function is assumed to be
continuously differentiable at least twice in each variable ($C^2$).
\\\\
Rearranging the model equations so that the first $m$ components are the
infected compartments, let $\mathcal{F}_i(x)$ be the rate of appearance
of new infections in compartment $i$, $\mathcal{V}_i^+(x)$ the rate of
transfer into compartment $i$ by all other means, and
$\mathcal{V}_i^-(x)$ the rate of transfer out of compartment $i$. The
model can then be written as
$$\dot{x}_i = f_i(x) = \mathcal{F}_i(x) - \mathcal{V}_i(x),
\ \ \ i = 1, \ldots, n,$$
with $\mathcal{V}_i(x) = \mathcal{V}_i^-(x) - \mathcal{V}_i^+(x)$. The
next-generation matrices are the Jacobians of these terms evaluated at
the disease-free equilibrium $x_0$:
$$F = \left[\frac{\partial \mathcal{F}_i(x_0)}{\partial x_j}\right],
\ \ \ V = \left[\frac{\partial \mathcal{V}_i(x_0)}{\partial x_j}\right],
\ \ \ 1 \leq i, j \leq m,$$
so that $\mathcal{R}_0 = \rho(FV^{-1})$.

\paragraph{Disease-free equilibrium and mosquito demographic equilibrium:}
\label{app:dfe}
At the DFE the human population is fully susceptible, $S_H^* = N$, and
all infected compartments vanish. The mosquito population rests at the
demographic equilibrium $M^*$ of the density-dependent recruitment
dynamics (11). Because the climate drivers are time-dependent, we
evaluate them at their fixed reference state (the study-period means
$\bar{T}, \bar{R}$), so that the recruitment rate and the carrying
capacity become constants:
$$
\bar{b} = b(\bar{R}, \bar{T}), \ \ \ 
\bar{K} = K(t)\big|_{\mathrm{DFE}} = M'\, f_T(\bar{T})\, f_R(\bar{R}),
$$
and
$$
\frac{dM}{dt} = \bar{b}\,\bar{K}\left(1 - \frac{M}{\bar{K}}\right) - \mu M
$$
Imposing $\Lambda(M^*) = \mu M^*$ gives
$$
\bar{b}\,\bar{K}\left(1 - \frac{M^*}{\bar{K}}\right) = \mu M^*
\ \ \ \Longrightarrow \ \ \ 
M^* = \frac{\bar{b}\,\bar{K}}{\bar{b} + \mu},
$$
and hence the recruitment at the DFE is
$$
\Lambda^* = \mu M^* = \frac{\mu\,\bar{b}\,\bar{K}}{\bar{b} + \mu}
$$
By Proposition~\ref{prop:persistence} (Section~\ref{sec:mosquito_pop_dynamics}), $M^* > 0$
is the unique relevant equilibrium whenever $\bar{b}, \bar{K} > 0$.
Therefore, the disease-free equilibrium will be
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dfe}
x_0 = \left(N,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ M^*,\ 0,\ 0\right)^\top.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Verification of $\mathcal{R}_0$ by explicit multiplication:}
\label{app:ngm_verify}

From \eqref{eq:ngm}, the transfer matrix is block-diagonal,
$V=\operatorname{diag}(V_H,V_M)$, with
$$
V_H=\begin{pmatrix} b_{3H} & 0 \\ -b_{3H} & \gamma \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
V_M=\begin{pmatrix} b_{3M}\ell+\mu & 0 \\ -b_{3M}\ell & \mu \end{pmatrix},
$$
so that
$$V^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}\big(V_H^{-1},V_M^{-1}\big),$$
$$V_H^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{b_{3H}} & 0 \\ \frac{1}{\gamma} & \frac{1}{\gamma} \end{pmatrix},$$
$$V_M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{b_{3M}\ell+\mu} & 0 \\ \frac{b_{3M}\ell}{\mu(b_{3M}\ell+\mu)} & \frac{1}{\mu} \end{pmatrix}$$
Carrying out the block multiplication $FV^{-1}$ yields the hollow form
$$FV^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & P \\ Q & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
$$P=\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{ab_2 S_H^* b_{3M}\ell}{N\mu(b_{3M}\ell+\mu)} & \frac{ab_2 S_H^*}{N\mu}\\
0 & 0
\end{pmatrix},$$
$$Q=\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{ab_1 S_M^*}{N\gamma} & \frac{ab_1 S_M^*}{N\gamma}\\
0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$
Both $P$ and $Q$ have rank one, so $PQ$ has a single nonzero eigenvalue
$\lambda=\frac{a^2 b_1 b_2 S_H^* S_M^* b_{3M}\ell}{N^2\mu\gamma(b_{3M}\ell+\mu)}$,
and the spectrum of $FV^{-1}$ is
$$
\sigma\big(FV^{-1}\big)=\Big\{0,\,0,\,\pm\sqrt{\lambda}\Big\}
=\Big\{0,\,0,\,\pm\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_0^H\,\mathcal{R}_0^M}\Big\}
$$
Substituting $S_H^*=N$ and $S_M^*=M^*=\Lambda^*/\mu$ into $\lambda$ and taking
the square root reproduces \eqref{eq:R0}.

\paragraph{Spectral computation of $\rho(FV^{-1})$:}
\label{app:eigen}

The matrix $V$ is block-diagonal in the human-mosquito partition, with blocks $V_H$ and $V_M$, so
$$
V^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{1}{b_{3H}} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[2mm]
\dfrac{1}{\gamma} & \dfrac{1}{\gamma} & 0 & 0 \\[2mm]
0 & 0 & \dfrac{1}{\mu + b_{3M}\ell} & 0 \\[2mm]
0 & 0 & \dfrac{b_{3M}\ell}{(\mu + b_{3M}\ell)\,\mu} & \dfrac{1}{\mu}
\end{pmatrix}
$$
Multiplying $F$ by $V^{-1}$ we obtain
$$
FV^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 & \dfrac{a b_2 b_{3M}\ell}{(\mu+b_{3M}\ell)\mu} & \dfrac{a b_2}{\mu} \\[2mm]
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\[2mm]
\dfrac{a b_1 M^*}{N\gamma} & \dfrac{a b_1 M^*}{N\gamma} & 0 & 0 \\[2mm]
0 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$
The second and fourth rows are zero, so the spectral problem reduces to
the $2\times2$ block coupling the human and mosquito exposed classes,
$$
\big[FV^{-1}\big]_{\{1,3\}} =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & \dfrac{a b_2 b_{3M}\ell}{(\mu + b_{3M}\ell)\mu} \\[2mm]
\dfrac{a b_1 M^*}{N\gamma} & 0
\end{pmatrix},
$$
whose characteristic equation is
$$
\lambda^2 =
\frac{a^2 b_1 b_2 b_{3M}\ell\, M^*}{N\gamma\,(\mu + b_{3M}\ell)\,\mu}
$$
The eigenvalues of $FV^{-1}$ are
$
\lambda \in \left\{\, 0,\ 0,\ \pm\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_H \mathcal{R}_M}\,\right\}$, where
$$\mathcal{R}_H = \frac{a b_2}{\gamma},
\ \ \ 
\mathcal{R}_M = \frac{a b_1 \Lambda^*}{N\mu}
\frac{b_{3M}\ell}{(b_{3M}\ell + \mu)\,\mu},
$$
so that $\mathcal{R}_0 = \rho(FV^{-1}) = \sqrt{\mathcal{R}_H \mathcal{R}_M}$.
Finally, substituting $\Lambda^* = \mu M^*$ recovers the expression in
Section~\ref{sec:basic_reproduction_number}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R0_appendix}
\mathcal{R}_0 =
\sqrt{\frac{a^2 b_1 b_2\, \Lambda^*\, b_{3M}\ell}
{N\gamma\,(b_{3M}\ell + \mu)\,\mu^2}} 
\end{equation}
